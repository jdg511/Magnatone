Magnatone True Vibrato: Bending Pitch Before It Was Possible

Illicit Apothecary · Circuits That Should Not Exist

Part of an ongoing series on the strange and beautiful circuits inside vintage audio gear. I design and build analog audio hardware here at Illicit Apothecary, and before I build anything I model it. Every moving graphic and every sound demo in this post is generated live by a model of the actual Magnatone circuit, so what you are seeing and hearing is the math of the real thing, not a recording.

The amp company that bent time

In 1957 nearly every "vibrato" knob on every amplifier in every music store in America was lying to you. Turn it up and the volume pulsed. The pitch of the note never moved a single cent. That effect is tremolo, and it is lovely, but it is not vibrato, and the industry had simply swapped the words. Fender stamped "vibrato" on channels that wobbled loudness, then stamped "synchronized tremolo" on the Stratocaster bridge, a device that actually bends pitch. Guitarists have been using both terms backwards ever since.[1]

Into that confusion walked a small California company best known for mother-of-pearl covered lap steels, and shipped something truly different: an amplifier whose built-in effect pushed the actual pitch of the note sharp and flat, smoothly, like a singer's throat. Magnatone was so proud of it that the company put a "V" emblem on the grille of every amp that had it, and two Vs on the stereo flagship.[2] Their sales literature called it F.M. Vibrato, for full modulation, and for once the marketing was underselling it.[3]

Why real vibrato was considered close to impossible

To understand why nobody else did this, think about what pitch shifting actually requires. Frequency is the rate at which a waveform's phase goes by. To raise the pitch of a signal passing through a box, the box has to deliver the waveform faster than it arrived, and to lower it, slower. A box that changes pitch is a box that manipulates time.

The honest way to do that is a modulated delay line: store the signal briefly, then read it back out while smoothly varying how far behind the write point you are reading. Sweep the delay shorter and pitch rises; sweep it longer and pitch falls. Every chorus and vibrato pedal on your board today works exactly this way. And in 1957 every known way of doing it was unavailable. Tape could store audio, but a tape loop with a wobbling playback speed was a studio trick, not something you could cram next to a 6V6 and sell for $179.50. The bucket brigade chip, the BBD that made Small Stones and Memory Men possible, would not be invented at Philips until 1969. Digital memory for audio was science fiction. There was, on paper, no way to build a time machine out of 1957 parts.

Magnatone's engineer found the loophole, and the loophole is phase. An all-pass filter passes every frequency at the same volume but delays each frequency by a different amount; the steeper its phase curve, the more delay it imposes around a given frequency. That is the sleight of hand: over a limited band, a swept phase-shift network behaves exactly like a modulated delay line. It stores no samples and has no memory, yet while its phase is in motion, every note inside its band comes out pitch-shifted, because a changing phase is indistinguishable from a changing frequency. The derivative of phase is frequency. Sweep 130 degrees of phase back and forth a few times a second and you are bending pitch by real, measurable cents, with nothing but a capacitor and a resistor that knows how to change its mind.

A patent, an organ company, and a resistor made of sand

The man who built it was Don L. Bonham, who became Magnatone's chief engineer in early 1957 and folded his vibrato work into the new Custom 200 series that same year.[4] He came from the organ world: he had been patenting phase-shift vibrato circuits since 1954 at Pacific Mercury, the company behind Thomas organs, and the group of businessmen who bought Magnatone in 1957 was led by F. Roy Chilton, who was involved with Thomas Organ himself. Two years later the same people bought the bankrupt Estey Organ company outright.[5] The core idea traces back to a 1953 oscilloscope measurement trick by Robert Moses: a resistance-capacitance bridge whose output phase swings as you vary the resistance arm. In October 1958 Bonham filed the patent that defines the Magnatone sound, number 2,988,706, granted in June 1961, with two stereo-vibrato patents following in March 1959. He appears to have left by mid-1961; Estey kept building the circuit under license and renamed it Stereo F.M. Vibrato.[5]

The missing piece was the thing that varies the resistance. A pot needs a hand to turn it. Bonham used varistors: little discs of sintered silicon carbide whose resistance collapses as the voltage across them rises. Feed them a slow throbbing voltage from a low frequency oscillator and they become a resistor that changes its mind several times a second, swinging over a range of roughly twenty five to one, with no moving parts and no light bulbs. The part Magnatone used is understood to be the Carborundum (later Kanthal Globar) 233BNR-32, and the only company still making silicon carbide varistors today is Metrosil.[5] Billy Gibbons, talking to Vintage Guitar decades later, called the varistor "an oddball device," which from a man with his gear collection is a professional diagnosis.[7]

And here is the part that makes a Magnatone sound like a Magnatone rather than like a modern digital vibrato. Silicon carbide is viciously non-linear, and the non-linearity is lopsided: the varistor curve is gentle at the bottom of its range and steep at the top, the tube driving it clips harder in one direction than the other, and the resistor feeding the varistors compresses the top of the sweep. Send a clean sine wave into that chain and what comes out the other end is warped: the pitch snaps upward quickly, overshoots, and then sags back down slowly. In the model below, at full depth and around 1 kHz, the upward bend is nearly three times the size of the downward one. It is less like a sine wave and more like a wrist.

A word for anyone hunting one of these amps: the varistors are the whole value of the thing, and they are not stable. Silicon carbide appears to be slightly hygroscopic, so decades of humidity shift the high-resistance end of the curve, two parts of the same type can differ by ten percent or more, and you cannot check one with a multimeter because the meter does not put enough voltage across it. Builders have cloned them from metal oxide varistor and resistor networks with decent results, but a sixty year old amp whose vibrato has gone shallow may just have had its varistors drift, or, worse, harvested.[5] The other camp disagrees. The archive that documents these amps says varistors rarely if ever fail, and that a dead vibrato is nearly always tubes, sockets, leaky coupling caps, or an oscillator that has stopped oscillating, so check all of that before you blame the sand. It also notes that the original schematics say only "varistor" with no part number, that Bonham himself described the original selection as slow and laborious, and that Estey sold off its remaining stock at factory moving sales between 1968 and 1972, which is why the real ones are now in collectors' drawers rather than on the market.[23]

Stereo on the two Magnatone circuits is the countersweep the 280 used; the Uni-Vibe never shipped in stereo, so its stereo setting is a what-if with the right channel a quarter cycle behind the left, and its "tremolo" position is the pedal's own Chorus mode: the swept signal mixed with dry, which is what makes it a phaser. Audio modulates a delay line with the modeled sweep, so the warp you see is the warp you hear.

Tony Price, who ran engineering at Estey from 1963 to 1965 and inherited the circuit from Bonham, had the plainest description of it: a triode's plate and cathode carry the same signal about 180 degrees apart, and the vibrato samples the output alternately from one and then the other at a rate set by the varistors, so the phase swings back and forth and the ear hears a Doppler-like shift.[23] Two details of the real circuit are worth savoring. First, the bridge has a corner near 1 kHz. Below it the circuit behaves like a true modulated delay line, so every note from a bass low B up through the guitar's midrange gets the same bend in cents; above it the phase shift runs out of room and the bend tapers off, which keeps the brittle top from sounding seasick. Drag the note slider in the widget and watch the readout: the numbers barely move until you climb past the twelfth fret, then they shrink. Second, the low frequency oscillator itself runs overdriven and slightly asymmetric by design, so the sweep arrives pre-bent before the varistors even touch it. None of this reads like an accident. It reads like voicing.

Stereo vibrato in 1957, before most people owned a stereo

The Custom 280 did not stop at bending pitch. It carried two complete power amplifiers, two output transformers, and two speakers, with a single LFO driving the two vibrato channels in opposition: as one side sweeps sharp, the other sweeps flat. The pitch does not just wobble, it moves through the room, rocking between the speakers. Magnatone marked the occasion with that second V on the grille.[2] Retrofret's description of the 280 is the best one-liner anyone has written about it: "the closest electrical approximation to a Leslie speaker effect ever built into a guitar amplifier."[8]

Sit with the date for a second. The first mass-market stereo LPs reached American stores in 1958. Magnatone was shipping a guitar amplifier with countersweeping stereo pitch modulation while the average record player could not yet say the word stereo.

The stereo amps that followed kept Bonham's eight-varistor layout, two cascaded modulator stages per side, and the later ones added a Stereo Vibrato switch that runs the two sides at the same phase for mono or out of phase for stereo. In the suitcase era that meant a 12DW7, two 12AU7s and eight varistors devoted to vibrato alone, and the sales literature called the result F.M. Stereo Vibrato; Bonham's own stereo patent frames the point of countersweeping as a way to "improve vibrato by reducing the effect of wandering pitch on reiterated tones," which is a lawyer's way of saying it swirls without going out of tune.[23] The archive that documents the M14 notes a lovely subtlety: because the varistors change slowly, the two countersweeping signals almost never cancel each other, which is why the stereo effect swirls instead of hollowing out.[20]

Every vintage Magnatone with the real thing

Magnatone built tube amps with this circuit from 1957 until roughly 1967, when the company turned to solid state on its way out of the business entirely; amp production was discontinued shortly after the 1969 Estey merger into Electro-Learner.[4] Everything below is from that original run. The quick field check when a mystery Maggie turns up: look for the V. One V, mono vibrato. Two Vs, stereo.[2] The cheap student models skipped the circuit, so not every vintage Magnatone has it. Reverb is a separate question: none of the 1950s amps had it. It arrived with the 400 series in 1961, and only on the dearer models.[20]

The transistor coda, 1967 to 1969

Magnatone's last act is almost unknown and truly strange. The tube Pro Series of 1966 had already slipped: the MP3 and MP5 replaced the varistors with a neon relaxation oscillator flashing a #1869 lamp onto a photocell, and that photocell merely turned the signal up and down. It was a tremolo wearing the vibrato name, the one time Magnatone itself used the word loosely.[23] Then, in November 1967, the company went solid state with the Brutes, and the real thing came back. The line topped out at the 300-watt-peak M35 "The Killer," two 15s, two horns, 105 pounds, a six-transistor spring reverb, and, the 1969 catalog insisted, "the only amplifier in the world with the thrilling effect of STEREO VIBRATO."[21] There were no varistors inside. Engineer Bill Layton kept the Moses bridge but replaced the silicon carbide with an incandescent lamp shining on photocells, the same swap the Schober organ company had made in 1963.[5][24]

The factory drawings survive, thanks to Don Garvin, an Estey engineer from 1968 to 1971 who scanned his own set, and the M35 schematic shows exactly how it was done. Vibrato board 35-0060 holds a transistor phase-shift oscillator with a 100k speed pot, an intensity pot, and a transistor that drives a #1869 lamp. Two cadmium sulfide photocells sit on the board under that lamp, each shunted by 100k and each forming the resistive arm of a bridge against a 0.01 microfarad capacitor, with a transistor phase splitter standing in for the triode, so the channel still gets two cascaded Moses stages. The second channel lives on a near-identical auxiliary board with its own lamp, fed from the first board's oscillator: two boards, two lamps, four photocells per amp.[24] A lamp has thermal lag and a photocell brightens fast and darkens slowly, so the sweep comes out smoother than the varistor version, and at fast rates it shallows and skews because the cell never fully recovers between cycles. The transistor modes in the widgets above use those values, with the lamp and photocell behaviour modeled from textbook data rather than measured from an amp. The 240SV accordion amp of November 1968 kept the stereo version, and production ran until late 1970, after which the brand was mothballed. The company went out still refusing to fake its vibrato.[4][24]

The Uni-Vibe, the cousin from Japan

While Estey's engineers were laying out the M35's lamp-and-photocell board in Torrance in 1967, an engineer in Japan was finishing the same idea in a different box. Fumio Mieda had designed phase-shift circuits for organs, and said later that his effect was meant to recreate the drifting, swirling sound of shortwave radio under atmospheric interference, not a Leslie, whatever the catalogs implied. Honey sold it in Japan in 1967 as the Vibra Chorus; when Honey folded, Shin-ei took it over, added a speed pedal, and Univox put it in American stores in 1968 as the Uni-Vibe, where Jimi Hendrix made it the sound of Woodstock.[25]

Open one up and the bones are Bonham's. Each of its four stages is a transistor phase splitter feeding a Moses bridge, with a cadmium sulfide photocell as the swept arm, four cells under one lamp inside a mirrored shroud, and a transistor phase-shift oscillator driving the lamp. That is the 1967 Magnatone board almost component for component. The differences are what make it a different instrument. Magnatone used two stages per channel, both with the same 0.01 microfarad capacitor, so the whole sweep happens around one corner frequency and comes out as a clean pitch bend. The Uni-Vibe staggers its four stages across the spectrum, 0.015, 0.22, 0.00047 and 0.0047 microfarads, so different parts of the note get bent at different moments and the sweep comes out lumpy; its lamp idles at half brightness so the intensity knob swings the cells both ways; and its Chorus switch sums the swept signal back with the dry one, which turns the moving phase into moving notches. That last trick is why the world calls the Uni-Vibe a phaser. Flip it to Vibrato, which removes the dry signal, and it is a four-stage Magnatone in a pedal, with a bend that the model above says grows alarmingly on low notes because the big 0.22 microfarad stage swings hardest down there.[25]

Was anybody copying anybody? There is no record of a dispute. Bonham's patents were American, Honey built in Japan, Mieda credited his own organ work, and by 1967 the optical phase stage was already in Schober organ kits and in Ampeg's Gemini amps, so the idea was in the air on both sides of the Pacific.[5] There was never a factory stereo Uni-Vibe either. R.G. Keen documented a modification that takes the inverted output of the last stage to a second mixer, so one channel's notches sit where the other's peaks are; the stereo setting in the widget is my own what-if instead, with the right channel's sweep a quarter cycle behind the left.[25]

The people who made it famous

The Magnatone story has a patient zero, and it is not who most people guess. Robert Ward, leader of Dayton's Ohio Untouchables, the band that later became the Ohio Players, bought a Magnatone at a local music store and built his entire guitar identity around its watery shimmer. That is his vibrato winding through the Falcons' 1962 gospel-soul landmark "I Found a Love," behind a young Wilson Pickett.[13]

Lonnie Mack heard Ward and went straight out and got his own. A year later came "Wham!", the 1963 instrumental that put Magnatone vibrato on the map forever and reportedly sold Stevie Ray Vaughan his first single.[19] Listen for the way the pitch leans into each held note rather than pulsing; that is the varistor bridge doing its job, and no tremolo amp of the day could have made that sound. Mack used Magnatone vibrato amps almost exclusively for the rest of his career.[14]

Buddy Holly picked up a Custom 280 in September 1958, only months before his death, and that stereo warble is part of the late Holly recordings' DNA. The amp itself survived him: his widow eventually gave it to Emmylou Harris, who recorded with it in the late 1980s.[15] Bo Diddley ran Magnatones too, and told Rolling Stone of his first one, It was a 25-watt Magnatone, and back then they said it was too loud.[22] Scotty Moore, the man who invented rock and roll guitar behind Elvis, kept a late-fifties 213 Troubadour at home for recording until the end.[9] And the amps never really left the stage: Neil Young has run a vintage 280 in his live rig for decades, with his tech feeding a padded-down Fender Deluxe output into it purely for that stereo vibrato,[17] and Alex Turner leaned on a vintage Custom 410 and a 480 for the vibrato-soaked textures all over Arctic Monkeys' AM era.[18]

Bending time with sand and wire

Every modern vibrato, chorus, and pitch shifter is a memory device: tape, buckets of capacitors, or RAM, all storing the past so it can be replayed at the wrong speed. Magnatone had none of those and shipped real pitch bending anyway, by noticing that phase is a back door into time, and that a disc of silicon carbide could be talked into opening it five times a second. When the varistors ran out they did it again with a light bulb and a photocell, and when the company ran out the idea went dormant for fifty years. There are not many circuits from 1957 that still cannot be bought in a convincing single-chip version. This is one of them.

Next up in this series: Fender's brownface harmonic tremolo, the other great misnamed modulation of the era, which splits the guitar in two and rocks the halves against each other. If the Magnatone bends time, the harmonic tremolo bends tone, and the circuit is just as sneaky.
