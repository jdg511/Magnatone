Every moving graphic and every sound on this page is generated live by a model of the real Magnatone circuit, so what you see and hear is the math of the thing, not a recording.

The amp company that bent time

In 1957 nearly every "vibrato" knob on every amplifier in America was lying. Turn it up and the volume pulsed; the pitch never moved a cent. That effect is tremolo, and the industry had simply swapped the words. Fender stamped "vibrato" on channels that wobbled loudness and "synchronized tremolo" on the Stratocaster bridge, which actually bends pitch, and guitarists have used both terms backwards ever since.[1]

Into that confusion walked a small California company best known for pearloid lap steels, and shipped an amplifier whose built-in effect pushed the actual pitch of the note sharp and flat, smoothly, like a singer's throat. Magnatone put a "V" emblem on the grille of every amp that had it, and two Vs on the stereo flagship.[2] The literature called it F.M. Vibrato, for full modulation, and for once the marketing was underselling.[3]

Why real vibrato was considered close to impossible

To understand why nobody else did this, think about what pitch shifting actually requires. Frequency is the rate at which a waveform's phase goes by. To raise the pitch of a signal passing through a box, the box has to deliver the waveform faster than it arrived; to lower it, slower. A box that changes pitch is a box that manipulates time.

The honest way to do that is a modulated delay line: store the signal briefly, then read it back while smoothly varying how far behind the write point you are reading. Every chorus and vibrato pedal on your board works this way. In 1957 every known way of doing it was unavailable. A tape loop with a wobbling playback speed was a studio trick, not something to cram next to a 6V6 and sell for $179.50. The bucket brigade chip, the BBD that made Small Stones and Memory Men possible, would not be invented at Philips until 1969. Digital memory for audio was science fiction. There was, on paper, no way to build a time machine out of 1957 parts.

Magnatone's engineer found the loophole, and the loophole is phase. An all-pass filter passes every frequency at the same volume but delays each by a different amount. Over a limited band, a swept phase-shift network behaves exactly like a modulated delay line: it stores nothing, yet while its phase is in motion every note inside its band comes out pitch-shifted, because a changing phase is indistinguishable from a changing frequency. The derivative of phase is frequency. Sweep 130 degrees of phase back and forth a few times a second and you are bending pitch by real, measurable cents, with nothing but a capacitor and a resistor that knows how to change its mind.

Pitch is how fast a waveform's phase goes by, so a box that raises pitch has to deliver the waveform faster than it arrived. A box that changes pitch is a box that manipulates time. The normal way to do that is a delay line whose delay is swept up and down, which is how every chorus and vibrato pedal works today. In 1957 none of the ways to build one existed: tape was a studio trick, the bucket brigade chip was twelve years away, and digital memory was science fiction.

Magnatone's loophole was phase. An all-pass filter passes every frequency at the same level but delays each by a different amount, and while its phase is being swept, every note inside its band comes out pitch-shifted, because a changing phase is indistinguishable from a changing frequency. Sweep 130 degrees of phase back and forth a few times a second and you get real, measurable cents of bend from nothing but a capacitor and a resistor that can change its own value.

A patent, an organ company, and a resistor made of sand

The man who built it was Don L. Bonham, Magnatone's chief engineer from early 1957, who folded his vibrato into the new Custom 200 series that year.[4] He came from the organ world, patenting phase-shift vibrato circuits since 1954 at Pacific Mercury, the company behind Thomas organs; the investors who bought Magnatone in 1957 were Thomas Organ people, and two years later they bought the bankrupt Estey Organ company outright.[5] The core idea traces to a 1953 oscilloscope trick by Robert Moses: a resistance-capacitance bridge whose output phase swings as you vary the resistance arm. In October 1958 Bonham filed the patent that defines the Magnatone sound, number 2,988,706, granted in June 1961, with two stereo patents following in 1959; he left around 1961 and Estey kept building the circuit under license, renaming it Stereo F.M. Vibrato.[5]

The missing piece was the thing that varies the resistance. A pot needs a hand to turn it. Bonham used varistors: little discs of sintered silicon carbide whose resistance collapses as the voltage across them rises. Feed them a slow throbbing voltage from a low frequency oscillator and they become a resistor that changes its mind several times a second, over a range of roughly twenty five to one, with no moving parts. The part is understood to be the Carborundum 233BNR-32; only Metrosil still makes silicon carbide varistors.[5] Billy Gibbons called the varistor "an oddball device," which from a man with his collection is a professional diagnosis.[7]

And here is why a Magnatone sounds like a Magnatone rather than a digital vibrato. Silicon carbide is viciously non-linear, and lopsidedly so: the curve is gentle at the bottom of its range and steep at the top, the tube driving it clips harder one way than the other, and the feed resistor compresses the top of the sweep. Send a clean sine into that chain and the pitch snaps upward quickly, overshoots, and sags back down slowly. In the model below, at full depth around 1 kHz, the upward bend is nearly three times the downward one. It is less like a sine wave and more like a wrist.

For anyone hunting one: silicon carbide is slightly hygroscopic, two parts of the same type can differ by ten percent, and a multimeter cannot check one because it puts too little voltage across it.[5] The archive that documents these amps counters that varistors rarely fail; a dead vibrato is nearly always tubes, sockets, leaky coupling caps, or a stopped oscillator, so check those before you blame the sand. Still, a sixty year old amp whose vibrato has gone shallow may simply have drifted varistors, or, worse, harvested ones. The original schematics say only "varistor" with no part number, Bonham himself described the original selection as slow and laborious, and Estey sold its remaining stock at factory moving sales between 1968 and 1972, which is why the real ones live in collectors' drawers.[23]

Stereo on the Magnatones is the 280 countersweep, left red, right orange. The Uni-Vibe never shipped in stereo, so it is mono and its second switch is the pedal's own: Vibrato is swept only, Chorus a 50/50 mix with dry.

Tony Price, who ran engineering at Estey from 1963 to 1965 and inherited the circuit from Bonham, had the plainest description: a triode's plate and cathode carry the same signal about 180 degrees apart, and the vibrato samples the output alternately from one and then the other at a rate set by the varistors, so the phase swings and the ear hears a Doppler-like shift.[23] Two details are worth savoring. The bridge has a corner near 1 kHz: below it the circuit is a true modulated delay line, so everything from a bass low B through the guitar's midrange gets the same bend in cents; above it the phase runs out of room and the bend tapers, which keeps the top end from sounding seasick. Drag the centre slider and watch the readout hold steady up to a few hundred hertz, then shrink. And the oscillator runs overdriven and slightly asymmetric, so the sweep arrives pre-bent. None of this reads like an accident. It reads like voicing.

Stereo vibrato in 1957, before most people owned a stereo

The Custom 280 carried two complete power amplifiers, two output transformers and two speakers, with a single LFO driving the two vibrato channels in opposition: as one side sweeps sharp, the other sweeps flat, and the pitch rocks between the speakers. Hence the second V on the grille.[2] Retrofret's one-liner is the best anyone has written: "the closest electrical approximation to a Leslie speaker effect ever built into a guitar amplifier."[8] Sit with the date for a second. The first mass-market stereo LPs reached stores in 1958. Magnatone was shipping countersweeping stereo pitch modulation while the average record player could not yet say the word.

The stereo amps that followed kept Bonham's eight-varistor layout, two cascaded stages per side, and later added a Stereo Vibrato switch that runs the sides in phase for mono or out of phase for stereo. In the suitcase era that meant a 12DW7, two 12AU7s and eight varistors devoted to vibrato alone. Bonham's stereo patent frames countersweeping as a way to "improve vibrato by reducing the effect of wandering pitch on reiterated tones," a lawyer's way of saying it swirls without going out of tune.[23] Because the varistors change slowly, the two signals almost never cancel, which is why the effect swirls instead of hollowing out.[20]

Every vintage Magnatone with the real thing

Magnatone built tube amps with this circuit from 1957 until roughly 1967, when the company went solid state on its way out of the business; production ended shortly after the 1969 Estey merger into Electro-Learner.[4] The field check when a mystery Maggie turns up: look for the V. One V, mono vibrato; two, stereo.[2] The cheap student models skipped the circuit. Reverb arrived with the 400 series in 1961, and only on the dearer models.[20]

The transistor coda, 1967 to 1969

The tube Pro Series of 1966 had already slipped: the MP3 and MP5 replaced the varistors with a neon oscillator flashing a lamp onto a photocell that merely turned the signal up and down, a tremolo wearing the vibrato name.[23] Then, in November 1967, Magnatone went solid state with the Brutes and the real thing came back. The line topped out at the 300-watt-peak M35 "The Killer," two 15s, two horns, 105 pounds, a six-transistor spring reverb, and, the 1969 catalog insisted, "the only amplifier in the world with the thrilling effect of STEREO VIBRATO."[21] There were no varistors inside. Engineer Bill Layton kept the Moses bridge but replaced the silicon carbide with an incandescent lamp shining on photocells, the swap the Schober organ company had made in 1963.[5][24]

The factory drawings survive thanks to Don Garvin, an Estey engineer who scanned his own set. Vibrato board 35-0060 holds a transistor phase-shift oscillator and a transistor driving a #1869 lamp over two cadmium sulfide photocells, each the resistive arm of a bridge against a 0.01 microfarad capacitor, with a transistor phase splitter standing in for the triode; a second board with its own lamp handles the other channel.[24] A lamp has thermal lag and a photocell brightens fast and darkens slowly, so the sweep is smoother than the varistor version, and at fast rates it shallows and skews because the cell never fully recovers. The transistor mode in the widgets uses those values, with the lamp and photocell behaviour modeled from textbook data rather than measured from an amp. The 240SV accordion amp of November 1968 kept the stereo version, and production ran until late 1970, after which the brand was mothballed. The company went out still refusing to fake its vibrato.[4][24]

The Uni-Vibe, the cousin from Japan

While Estey was laying out the M35's board in Torrance in 1967, Fumio Mieda in Japan was finishing the same idea in a different box. Mieda had designed phase-shift circuits for organs, and said later that his effect was meant to recreate the drifting, swirling sound of shortwave radio under atmospheric interference, not a Leslie, whatever the catalogs implied. Honey sold it in Japan in 1967 as the Vibra Chorus; when Honey folded, Shin-ei took it over, added a speed pedal, and Univox put it in American stores in 1968 as the Uni-Vibe, where Jimi Hendrix made it the sound of Woodstock.[25]

Open one up and the bones are Bonham's: four transistor phase splitters each feeding a Moses bridge, a cadmium sulfide photocell as the swept arm, four cells under one lamp in a mirrored shroud, and a transistor oscillator driving the lamp. That is the 1967 Magnatone board almost component for component. The differences make it a different instrument. Magnatone used two stages per channel with the same 0.01 microfarad capacitor, so the whole sweep happens around one corner and comes out as a clean pitch bend. The Uni-Vibe staggers its four stages across the spectrum, 0.015, 0.22, 0.00047 and 0.0047 microfarads, so different parts of the note bend at different moments and the sweep comes out lumpy; its lamp idles at half brightness so the intensity knob swings the cells both ways; and its Chorus switch sums the swept signal with the dry one, turning moving phase into moving notches. That is why the world calls it a phaser. Flip it to Vibrato, which drops the dry signal, and it is a four-stage mono Magnatone in a pedal, with a bend that grows alarmingly at low frequencies because the 0.22 microfarad stage swings hardest down there.[25] Was anyone copying anyone? There is no record of a dispute: Bonham's patents were American, Honey built in Japan, Mieda credited his own organ work, and by 1967 the optical phase stage was already in Schober organ kits and Ampeg's Gemini amps, so the idea was in the air on both sides of the Pacific.[5]

The people who made it famous

The story has a patient zero, and it is not who most people guess. Robert Ward, leader of Dayton's Ohio Untouchables, later the Ohio Players, bought a Magnatone at a local music store and built his guitar identity around its watery shimmer. That is his vibrato winding through the Falcons' 1962 landmark "I Found a Love," behind a young Wilson Pickett.[13] Lonnie Mack heard Ward and got his own, and a year later came "Wham!", the 1963 instrumental that put Magnatone vibrato on the map and reportedly sold Stevie Ray Vaughan his first single.[19] Listen for the way the pitch leans into each held note rather than pulsing; no tremolo amp of the day could do that. Mack used Magnatones almost exclusively for the rest of his career.[14]

Buddy Holly picked up a Custom 280 in September 1958, months before his death, and that stereo warble is in the DNA of the late Holly recordings; his widow later gave the amp to Emmylou Harris, who recorded with it.[15] Bo Diddley ran Magnatones too, and told Rolling Stone of his first one, It was a 25-watt Magnatone, and back then they said it was too loud.[22] Scotty Moore, the man who invented rock and roll guitar behind Elvis, kept a late-fifties 213 Troubadour at home for recording until the end.[9] Neil Young has run a vintage 280 in his live rig for decades, with his tech feeding a padded-down Fender Deluxe output into it purely for that stereo vibrato,[17] and Alex Turner leaned on a vintage Custom 410 and a 480 for the vibrato-soaked textures all over Arctic Monkeys' AM era.[18]

Bending time with sand and wire

Every modern vibrato, chorus and pitch shifter is a memory device: tape, buckets of capacitors, or RAM, storing the past so it can be replayed at the wrong speed. Magnatone had none of those and shipped real pitch bending anyway, by noticing that phase is a back door into time and that a disc of silicon carbide could be talked into opening it five times a second. When the varistors ran out they did it again with a light bulb and a photocell, and when the company ran out the idea went dormant for fifty years. There are not many circuits from 1957 that still cannot be bought in a convincing single-chip version. This is one of them.

Next in this series: Fender's brownface harmonic tremolo, the other great misnamed modulation of the era, which splits the guitar in two and rocks the halves against each other. If the Magnatone bends time, the harmonic tremolo bends tone, and the circuit is just as sneaky.
